Who Is Bezalel?

By Ephraim Kalish

There is a man in the Bible whose name is known to relatively few, although his calling holds a unique place in the relationship between God and humanity. His name is Bezalel—the artist whom God appointed to create the Mishkan, the sanctuary of His presence among Israel.

“See, I have called by name Bezalel the son of Uri, the son of Hur, of the tribe of Judah: And I have filled him with the spirit of God, in wisdom, and in understanding, and in knowledge, and in all manner of workmanship.” Exodus 31:2–3, KJV

Bezalel is the first person in Scripture explicitly described as being filled with the Spirit of God. Remarkably, that person is an artist.

In Hebrew, his abilities are described by the words chokhmah (חָכְמָה)—wisdom, tevunah (תְּבוּנָה)—understanding, and da'at (דַּעַת)—knowledge. These are more than a list of professional skills. Scripture uses these same concepts to describe the divine wisdom through which God established the world (Proverbs 3:19–20).

Bezalel was to work with gold, silver, bronze, stone, wood, and textiles. But before beginning his work, he had to receive a design that did not originate with him. Moses had been commanded to make the sanctuary according to the pattern God had shown him on the mountain (Exodus 25:9, 40).

Here we encounter the distinctive nature of Bezalel's creative calling: the invisible was to become visible, and a divine design was to take material form.

The name Bezalel (בְּצַלְאֵל) means “in the shadow of God.” A shadow cannot exist on its own: it requires light and something that casts it. It testifies to the existence of the original without being the original itself. This image offers a striking expression of the artist's relationship to the Creator.

Yet Bezalel was more than an executor of instructions. God also placed within his heart the ability to teach others (Exodus 35:34). His gift was therefore not confined to one individual but passed on to other craftsmen who shared in the work.

The Bible records not a single speech by Bezalel. His testimony was expressed through the works he created at God's command. In this particular sense, he may be understood as a prophet who prophesied through his art. This is not a biblical title given to Bezalel, but an interpretation of the nature of his service.

Bezalel's story reveals a truth of fundamental importance to artists: serving God does not necessarily require words. Artistic creation can be more than an illustration of a sermon; it can itself become a way of bearing witness to the invisible.

That is why the question “Who is Bezalel?” reaches far beyond the history of ancient Israel. It is a question about the nature of artistic gifts, their source, and their purpose.
